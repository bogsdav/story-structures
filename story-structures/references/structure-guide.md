# Structure guide

Contents: A. Hero’s Journey · B. Three Acts · C. Save the Cat · D. Snowflake · E. Freytag · F. Seven Points · G. Story Circle.

Definitions below are concise paraphrases; framework names retain their conventional labels. Examples, diagnostic questions, fit judgments and adaptation advice are original editorial applications. Consult sources.md for attribution. These tools organize different things: a mythic pattern, dramatic architecture, beat design or a development process. They are not interchangeable checklists.

## A. Hero’s Journey / Monomyth

### What it organizes
Campbell describes patterns in myth, especially departure (also called separation), initiation and return. The familiar 17 headings organize possible motifs, not 17 mandatory incidents. The Joseph Campbell Foundation explicitly warns against treating all motifs as compulsory sequential steps. A writing adaptation should preserve the distinction between this account and a prescriptive screenplay template.

### Campbell’s commonly listed 17 motifs

| Movement | Motif | Basic function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Departure | Call to Adventure | An invitation or disruption opens another way of life. |
| Departure | Refusal of the Call | Resistance exposes attachment or fear. |
| Departure | Supernatural Aid | Assistance equips the traveler. |
| Departure | Crossing the First Threshold | Entry into unfamiliar conditions. |
| Departure | Belly of the Whale | Separation from an old identity. |
| Initiation | Road of Trials | Repeated testing. |
| Initiation | Meeting with the Goddess | Encounter with fullness or sustaining love. |
| Initiation | Woman as Temptress | Attraction diverts the quest. |
| Initiation | Atonement with the Father | Confrontation with ultimate authority. |
| Initiation | Apotheosis | Expanded understanding or transformed being. |
| Initiation | Ultimate Boon | Attainment of the sought gift. |
| Return | Refusal of the Return | Resistance to rejoining ordinary life. |
| Return | Magic Flight | Escape with the gift. |
| Return | Rescue from Without | Outside help enables return. |
| Return | Crossing the Return Threshold | Reconciliation of extraordinary and ordinary experience. |
| Return | Master of Two Worlds | Capacity to inhabit both. |
| Return | Freedom to Live | Release from limiting fear. |

Historical labels such as “Woman as Temptress” reflect gendered assumptions; they are not instructions to make women obstacles or men heroes. If recasting that motif as temptation, identify it as a contemporary adaptation. Likewise, an authority encounter need not involve a literal father. Do not erase the original terminology when explaining history or attribute inclusive reinterpretations to Campbell himself.

### Vogler’s 12-stage writing adaptation

| Stage | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Ordinary World | Establish everyday conditions. |
| Call to Adventure | Introduce the challenge. |
| Refusal of the Call | Expose reluctance. |
| Meeting with the Mentor | Provide preparation or guidance. |
| Crossing the First Threshold | Commit to unfamiliar conditions. |
| Tests, Allies, Enemies | Discover capabilities and relationships. |
| Approach to the Inmost Cave | Prepare for concentrated danger. |
| Ordeal | Undergo a major crisis. |
| Reward | Gain something through the ordeal. |
| The Road Back | Face the demands of return. |
| Resurrection | Demonstrate transformation in a final test. |
| Return with the Elixir | Bring the gain into ordinary life. |

Vogler develops a practical writing model from Campbell. An ordeal and a late resurrection test have different dramatic jobs; do not merge them automatically. Mentors may be functions rather than single characters. Literal resurrection, magic and travel are not required in a modern adaptation.

### Use and limits
- Helps: connect an external undertaking with identity change and reintegration. Plausible when the author cares about crossing boundaries and bringing something back.
- May distort: a distributed ensemble, a deliberately static observer, a story about remaining in place, or a culture-specific narrative whose pattern differs. Do not claim universality across cultures.
- Common mistakes: inserting a mentor death, making every conflict heroic, stretching a brief story to 17 stages, or treating Campbell and Vogler as the same list.
- Defining versus flexible: departure/initiation/return supplies the broad mythic movement; motif count, literal travel, helper identity and stage duration are flexible. A substantially broken return pattern should be labeled an adaptation.
- Original example: A ferry keeper crosses a forbidden inlet to repair a warning bell. Dependence on local guides overturns her belief that competence means working alone. She returns with a repaired bell and a shared watch schedule. This illustrates departure, testing and reintegration; the schedule embodies change without a coronation or victory parade.
- Ask: What boundary matters? What old strategy fails? What can the protagonist bring back? Does returning matter to this story, or are we adding it for the template?

## B. Three-Act Structure

### What it organizes and its functions
Three Acts divides a dramatic movement into setup, confrontation and resolution. It is a broad architecture; Syd Field’s paradigm is one influential screenplay implementation, not the only definition.

| Part or turn | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Act I: Setup | Establish situation, desires, pressures and a developing central question. |
| Inciting incident | Disturb existing conditions and initiate the story problem. |
| First act break | Commit the story to a changed course; often a decision or irreversible consequence. |
| Act II: Confrontation | Develop attempts, resistance, discoveries and consequences. |
| Midpoint | Often reframe the problem through revelation, reversal, commitment or apparent success. |
| Second act break | Create the conditions for the final movement. |
| Crisis | Make alternatives or incompatible values acute. |
| Act III: Resolution | Bring the central dramatic question to an outcome. |
| Climax | Deliver the decisive confrontation, choice, recognition or event. |
| Denouement | Show relevant aftermath and the resulting condition. |

An inciting incident can precede the commitment by several scenes: receiving an eviction notice starts a problem; deciding to contest it redirects the story. They can coincide, but the functions differ. Crisis and climax may occur in one scene. A midpoint need not be a twist. The familiar quarter/half/quarter proportions are planning aids, not deadlines; act boundaries need not be labeled chapters or equal units.

### Use and limits
- Helps: clarify what starts, develops and resolves a central movement without specifying every scene. Good for writers seeking a loose container.
- May distort: an intentionally open constellation of scenes if “resolution” is mistaken for closure of every question.
- Common mistakes: an exposition-only first act; a second act of unrelated delays; calling the entire final act the climax; using a percentage to justify an unmotivated turn.
- Defining versus flexible: distinct dramatic phases are the organizing idea. Exact proportions, midpoint type, number of subplots and nature of resolution are flexible.
- Original example: A tenant learns her building will be sold and decides to organize a meeting. Conflicting needs fracture the coalition, and a failed petition reveals that residents want different outcomes. She brokers separate relocation and purchase options; the final vote ends the shared campaign while leaving friendships unsettled.
- Ask: What changes the story’s direction? Does each attempt affect the next? What is resolved emotionally or practically? Is the middle developing the problem or merely postponing it?

## C. Save the Cat

### What it organizes
Blake Snyder’s screenplay approach coordinates 15 beats. Jessica Brody adapts it for novels. A “save the cat” moment is an audience-engagement device that can make a character worth following; it is not the entire beat sheet and need not involve an animal or saintly behavior.

### Beats and dramatic functions

| Beat | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Opening Image | Establish the starting condition visually or concretely. |
| Theme Stated | Introduce an idea the story will test. |
| Setup | Establish ordinary patterns and vulnerabilities. |
| Catalyst | Disturb the existing arrangement. |
| Debate | Explore reluctance, options or preparation. |
| Break into Two | Enter a changed course. |
| B Story | Develop a relationship or thread carrying thematic pressure. |
| Fun and Games | Explore the premise’s distinctive possibilities. |
| Midpoint | Change the apparent direction or stakes. |
| Bad Guys Close In | Intensify external or internal pressure. |
| All Is Lost | Collapse the current approach or apparent prospects. |
| Dark Night of the Soul | Process that collapse. |
| Break into Three | Find a new way forward. |
| Finale | Put the new approach into action. |
| Final Image | Reveal the resulting condition. |

“Fun” names the premise’s appeal, not necessarily a cheerful mood. “Bad guys” can be pressures rather than villains. The B Story need not be romantic. A beat may be a moment, scene or sequence.

### Pacing distinction
Snyder’s original familiar targets assume a screenplay around 110 pages: for example Catalyst around page 12, Break into Two around 25, Midpoint around 55 and Break into Three around 85. Brody uses manuscript percentages, including Catalyst near 10%, Break into Two near 20%, Midpoint near 50%, All Is Lost near 75% and Break into Three near 80%. Do not convert screenplay pages directly into novel pages. Novel word-count proportions are more stable than formatted page counts. The targets are diagnostic guides in this skill, never compulsory cutoffs.

### Use and limits
- Helps: identify a missing change in strategy, an underdeveloped premise or a weak relationship between plot and character movement. Plausible for a writer who wants detailed checkpoints.
- May distort: a short story, flat arc or restrained literary work if it acquires artificial reversals and an imposed lesson merely to satisfy all 15 labels.
- Common mistakes: treating Theme Stated as a sermon; making All Is Lost require a death; inserting a love interest for B Story; confusing a beat with a chapter.
- Defining versus flexible: the named sequence supplies a recognizable model. Its event scale, placement and literal expressions are adaptable. Omitting or reordering key functions should be named as an adaptation rather than presented as an exact sheet.
- Original example: A meticulous cook enters a communal supper contest to prove she needs no help. A freezer failure forces collaboration. Her apparent success encourages her to take sole credit, and her helpers withdraw. She admits the dependence, redesigns the menu with them and serves a modest meal under everyone's names. Opening and final images contrast a locked kitchen with an open worktable.
- Ask: What promise makes the premise attractive? What does apparent success conceal? Does the final strategy follow from experience? Would an added low point deepen this particular story or falsify its tone?

## D. Snowflake Method

### What it organizes
Randy Ingermanson’s method expands an idea through progressively detailed planning. It is a development process, not a plot architecture. Its commonly taught ten steps are:

1. Write a one-sentence story summary.
2. Expand it to a paragraph covering setup, major disasters and ending.
3. Summarize major characters, including motivations, goals, conflict and change.
4. Expand the story paragraph into a page.
5. Describe the story from individual characters’ perspectives.
6. Expand the page into a longer synopsis, commonly four pages.
7. Develop fuller character charts and histories.
8. Derive a scene list, often in a spreadsheet.
9. Expand scenes into narrative descriptions; this step is optional in the author's later practice.
10. Write the first draft.

Earlier work is revisable as later detail exposes problems. The method’s disaster-oriented paragraph is a planning convention, not evidence that every kind of fiction needs three catastrophes.

### Use and limits
- Helps: turn disconnected material into progressively testable decisions; exposes contradictions before drafting. Plausible for an author who likes alternating character and plot development.
- Pairing: use Three Acts for major phases, Seven Points for decisive turns, or the Circle for desire/cost/return. Snowflake controls the order of development; the selected architecture controls the resulting narrative arrangement.
- May distort: an exploratory writer’s process if every step becomes compulsory homework, or quiet fiction if the word “disaster” is interpreted literally.
- Common mistakes: offering Snowflake as if its ten steps were ten scenes; declaring the manuscript complete at step nine; expanding length without adding causality; refusing to revisit a prior summary.
- Defining versus flexible: progressive expansion and interaction between character and plot define the method. Exact page counts, tools and the optional scene-description step can change. A shortened version should be identified as such.
- Original example: Start with “A retiring locksmith must choose who inherits her workshop.” Use a three-act container: announce retirement, test competing arrangements, make a decision. Expand the summary only after clarifying why she distrusts each candidate. A scene list can then expose whether the eventual choice has been earned.
- Ask: Is the difficulty choosing events or organizing development? How much planning is helpful before drafting? Which new detail changes the one-sentence promise? What architecture is the expanded material actually following?

## E. Freytag’s Pyramid

### Historical model and functions
Freytag’s nineteenth-century dramatic model concerns an ascent and counter-movement with a central high point. It should not be collapsed into the common classroom diagram that labels a late decisive confrontation “climax.”

| Principal part | Function in the historical model |
| --- | --- |
| Introduction | Establish conditions from which dramatic action can arise. |
| Rise / rising action | Develop the movement toward the central height. |
| Climax | Culminate the ascending movement and enable reversal or counteraction. |
| Fall / falling action | Develop the consequences and counter-movement. |
| Catastrophe | Complete the tragic outcome. |

Three connecting dramatic forces are the exciting force (between introduction and rise), the tragic force (at the transition from climax toward falling action), and the force of final suspense (before catastrophe). The first launches action; the second turns achievement toward its consequences; the third briefly revives a possibility of avoiding the end. Freytag treats the exciting force as necessary and the other two as optional devices. The five parts are dramatic functions, not a rule that every adaptation must have five equal acts.

In simplified modern classroom usage the parts often become exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and resolution. That diagram can be useful, but its late climax and brief aftermath describe a different emphasis. Falling action in the historical model can grow more urgent: the character's prospects fall while the audience's suspense increases.

### Use and limits
- Helps: shape a tragedy whose apparent triumph creates the conditions of loss. Plausible when the consequence of success deserves as much space as its pursuit.
- May distort: a mystery whose main revelation needs to occur late, or an episodic work without a sustained tragic counter-movement.
- Common mistakes: calling falling action uneventful cleanup; applying the central climax to a modern action film without explanation; requiring a death merely because “catastrophe” appears in the model.
- Defining versus flexible: the rise, central culmination and consequential fall distinguish the historical pattern. A nontragic ending or a late apex is a labeled adaptation. Pacing ratios and scene counts remain flexible.
- Original example: A councilor conceals a faulty dam report to win funding. Her central victory secures construction. The resulting expansion overwhelms the dam, and her attempts to protect the project discredit the engineers who could save it. A final offer to confess arrives too late to prevent the failure. Public success has causally produced public ruin.
- Ask: What success plants the downfall? What changes direction at the central height? Does the latter half develop consequences? Is a final hope meaningful or merely a delay?

## F. Seven-Point Story Structure

### What it organizes
Dan Wells popularized this seven-turn approach; in Writing Excuses he says he acquired it from a role-playing book. Do not credit him as its inventor.

| Point | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Establish the initial condition. |
| Plot Turn One | Introduce the change that launches the main movement. |
| Pinch One | Apply pressure that requires a response. |
| Midpoint | Shift from reacting toward purposeful action. |
| Pinch Two | Increase pressure or remove support. |
| Plot Turn Two | Supply the understanding or means for the final action. |
| Resolution | Carry out the outcome and reveal the resulting condition. |

A useful planning order starts with Resolution, then contrasts Hook, establishes Midpoint and fills turns and pinches. This is planning backward, not a requirement to narrate backward. The seven points are major functions, not the only seven events.

### Use and limits
- Helps: repair a vague middle and connect the ending to earlier decisions. Plausible when the ending is known but the route is unclear.
- Subplots: map a separate seven-point trajectory only when helpful; stagger its turns and connect its consequences to the shared plot. Do not manufacture seven scenes for every minor character.
- May distort: a contemplative story if “reaction to action” is forced into physical assertiveness, or a negative arc if Resolution is assumed to mean victory.
- Common mistakes: killing a mentor to satisfy a pinch; dropping a convenient solution at Plot Turn Two; mechanically making opening and ending opposites in every respect.
- Defining versus flexible: the sequence of pressure, strategic change and resolution is the recognizable architecture. The pressures can be relational or internal. A flat arc can contrast external conditions rather than beliefs. A tragic adaptation can culminate in the capacity to make the fatal choice.
- Original example: A junior archivist avoids disputes. A deletion order threatens a collection; a failed appeal shows that courtesy alone cannot protect it. She starts assembling a public provenance record, loses access, then realizes that previously deposited copies establish ownership. She uses them at the hearing and saves the collection, having become willing to disagree publicly.
- Ask: What is the ending condition? What must the protagonist understand before reaching it? Which pressure changes behavior? Has the final resource been prepared rather than bestowed?

## G. Dan Harmon’s Story Circle

### What it organizes
Harmon's eight-position circle links a character's pursuit to unfamiliar experience, cost and return. Common shorthand is You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change.

| Position | Function |
| --- | --- |
| You | Establish a character in familiar circumstances. |
| Need | Identify something wanted or lacking. |
| Go | Cross into an unfamiliar situation. |
| Search | Adapt through attempts. |
| Find | Reach the object of pursuit or an apparent equivalent. |
| Take | Experience its price or consequences. |
| Return | Re-enter familiar circumstances. |
| Change | Reveal the resulting difference. |

“Need” does not automatically mean a hidden psychological wound; distinguish conscious desire from any inferred deeper need. Familiarity may be miserable rather than comfortable. Attainment and its price have different functions. Observable change can involve behavior, relationships, status or knowledge; it need not be moral improvement.

Harmon's television discussion allows a return toward the recurring arrangement. Preserve an episode's consequences even when the broader situation continues. Deterioration, a small adjustment or renewed commitment to an unstable status quo are usable adaptations; explain the deviation from a strong transformation reading.

### Use and limits
- Helps: connect what someone wants with what getting it costs. Plausible for a compact comic episode or a recurring character whose normal coping strategy creates trouble.
- May distort: a dispersed ensemble if a single “You” is artificially imposed, or a story whose interest lies in refusing a quest rather than completing a cycle.
- Common mistakes: confusing Find with the final ending; treating Take as another acquisition; insisting on redemption; claiming a character has changed without showing evidence.
- Defining versus flexible: descent into unfamiliar conditions and consequential return supply the organizing motion. Literal travel, degree of change, cost and scale are flexible. A genuinely unchanged protagonist may require change in the surrounding situation or a clearly labeled variation.
- Original example: A fussy roommate wants a silent dinner and reserves a private restaurant room. Discovering a broken speaker, he becomes the evening’s self-appointed technician. He finally gets silence by cutting the wrong circuit, also stopping the kitchen. Back home he orders takeout and permits one quiet song, while insisting the evening was a success. The adjustment is small enough to preserve a comic recurring character.
- Ask: What does the character think will fix things? What unfamiliar behavior follows? What does success cost? What is visibly different on returning, even if the character denies it?
