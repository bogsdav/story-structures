# Selection and comparison

These are editorial heuristics, not empirical predictions. Start with the user's intended experience. A genre label alone does not establish fit.

## A practical decision sequence

1. Identify the request: selecting an architecture, developing material, diagnosing an existing arrangement, or executing an already chosen structure.
2. Protect the supplied ending, tone, cast, world rules and other fixed decisions.
3. Name the actual difficulty: missing direction, weak causality, insufficient development, incoherent revelation order, thin motivation or a mismatch between form and expectations.
4. Choose the lightest architecture that addresses that difficulty. Add a development process only if it solves a separate problem.
5. Compare alternatives using identical canon and equivalent amounts of invention.
6. Apply the choice gate in SKILL.md. Stop before full development unless already authorized.

## Fit matrix

| Story need | Plausible starting option | Different useful option | Main caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identity tested through departure and return | Vogler or Campbell-inspired journey | Story Circle for a tighter desire/cost cycle | Do not invent travel, mentors or heroism. |
| Broad causal movement with room to experiment | Three Acts | Custom arrangement around changes in perception | Do not confuse flexible resolution with a mandatory happy ending. |
| Detailed plot-and-character checkpoints | Save the Cat | Seven Points for fewer prescribed functions | Do not add a low point merely to fill a box. |
| Success itself produces downfall | Historical Freytag | Three Acts with a negative arc | Distinguish central culmination from late decisive climax. |
| Known ending, unclear route | Seven Points | Three Acts with backward causal planning | A final solution needs preparation. |
| Desire creates a comic cost and return | Story Circle | Three Acts for a simpler episode spine | Small change is sufficient when observable. |
| Fragments need progressive development | Snowflake plus a selected architecture | Direct scene arrangement with periodic summaries | Snowflake is a process, not a rival plot shape. |
| Quiet observation or associative literary work | Flexible Three Acts only if phases emerge | Labeled custom pattern of encounters, recurrence or perception | Do not fabricate stakes or a villain to justify structure. |
| Nonlinear investigation | Three Acts organizing revelation | Seven Points organizing investigative decisions | Track chronology and reader knowledge separately. |
| Ensemble with intersecting outcomes | Shared Three-Act spine plus selective individual arcs | Custom braided arrangement | Do not give everyone the same arc or equal page time. |

## Fair comparison example

Fixed premise: Mira, a night librarian, discovers that a scheduled archive closure will erase unsent letters. She wants to preserve them. No ending, antagonist or supernatural mechanism is established.

- Three Acts: discovery and decision; attempts to preserve the letters and their consequences; an unresolved final choice about preservation. Emphasizes practical causality. Requires the closure mechanism and ending. Risks underplaying a meaningful return to ordinary life if that matters to the author.
- Story Circle: familiar library routine; desire to save the letters; unfamiliar pursuit; attempts; provisional attainment; its unknown cost; return; resulting difference. Emphasizes what preservation costs Mira. Requires a price and return that the premise has not supplied. These are open design decisions, not existing canon.
- A journey adaptation is not automatically a strong third option. If the author sees no boundary crossing or transformation, offer two options rather than manufacturing an adventure.

Ask: “Would you rather center the practical preservation problem (Three Acts) or the cost of getting what Mira wants (Story Circle)?” Do not give either option a better villain, stronger romance or more interesting ending just to make it win.

## Arc and form safeguards

Positive, negative, flat, ambiguous and collective arcs are legitimate. A flat protagonist can change the world or expose the cost of refusing to change. Ambiguity needs intelligible alternatives, not missing causal information disguised as sophistication.

For nonlinear work, create an event ledger with event ID, chronological position, disclosure position, viewpoint knowledge and consequence. A late flashback can reframe an early event without changing what happened. Flag information withheld implausibly from a viewpoint character.

For ensembles, use a shared conflict plus rows for each principal character's want, action, consequence and ending condition. Connect arcs through events; avoid isolated mini-novels that happen to share a location.

For a series, distinguish the current installment's answer from the unresolved series question. An episode's Circle can sit inside a season's Three Acts without implying every scale requires every beat. State one primary architecture at each scale.

For a custom arrangement, name its organizing principle plainly: for example “five encounters that progressively alter the narrator's understanding.” This is an editorial proposal, not an established named theory. Explain what the reader can track and why a standard architecture would be intrusive.
